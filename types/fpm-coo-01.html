Finding the length between two points

Using the distance formula to find a length between two points, often leaving the answer as a simplified surd, and using those lengths to compare sides or form a ratio.

Knowledge card

Finding the length between two points

Calculation
  • The distance between $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$.
  • The formula is Pythagoras' theorem: the change in $x$ and the change in $y$ are the two shorter sides of a right-angled triangle.
  • Squaring removes signs, so it does not matter which point you call the first point.
  • An exact length is left as a simplified surd: take out the largest square factor, e.g. $\sqrt{80} = \sqrt{16 \times 5} = 4\sqrt{5}$.
  • A triangle is isosceles when two of its side lengths are equal; you only need to compare $d^2$ values to show this.
  • In a right-angled triangle, $\tan \theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$, so lengths from coordinates can give a trig ratio.

Before you begin

  • Pythagoras' theorem.
  • Plotting and reading coordinates, including negative ones.
  • Simplifying surds, e.g. $\sqrt{72} = 6\sqrt{2}$.
  • Subtracting negative numbers.
1

The method

  1. Write down the two points and find the change in $x$ and the change in $y$, taking care with negative coordinates (e.g. $3 - (-4) = 7$).

    Why: These are the two shorter sides of the right-angled triangle under the line.

  2. Square each change, add them, and take the square root: $d = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2}$.

    Why: This is Pythagoras' theorem for the hypotenuse, which is the length you want.

  3. Simplify the surd by taking out the largest square factor, e.g. $\sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}$.

    Why: 'Exact' means a surd, not a decimal, and examiners expect the simplest form.

  4. If the question compares lengths (isosceles, ratio, scale factor or a trig ratio), use the lengths you found to make that comparison and write a conclusion.

    Why: The length is often only a tool; the marks are for the comparison asked for.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Sign slips when a coordinate is negative, e.g. writing $3 - (-4)$ as $-1$.
  • ✗
    Adding the coordinates instead of subtracting them, or forgetting to square one of the changes.
  • ✗
    Leaving the answer as $\sqrt{20}$ instead of the simplified $2\sqrt{5}$, or giving $4.47$ when an exact answer is asked.
  • ✗
    Writing $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = a + b$.
  • ✗
    In a show-that, calculating the lengths but not stating the conclusion.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from typical examiner credit patterns for this method; not measured student-error statistics.

Now see it used on a real past-paper question

What a complete response includes

Each length usually earns a method mark for a correct substitution into the distance formula (or a correct Pythagoras set-up) and an accuracy mark for the exact, simplified value. In a show-that, the final mark needs both equal lengths and a written conclusion. When lengths feed a ratio or trig value, a further method mark is given for using the lengths correctly and an accuracy mark for the simplified answer.

Finding the length between two points · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Question 8 · part (a) · 4 marks

The point $A$ has coordinates $(2, 6)$, the point $B$ has coordinates $(6, 8)$ and the point $C$ has coordinates $(4, 2)$. (a) Find the exact length of (i) $AB$ (ii) $BC$ (iii) $AC$.

Question restated by olevels.com from the original paper; the original is below.

View full original question and diagrams
After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. $AB = \sqrt{(6-2)^2 + (8-6)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 4} = \sqrt{20}$
  2. $\sqrt{20} = \sqrt{4 \times 5} = 2\sqrt{5}$
  3. $BC = \sqrt{(4-6)^2 + (2-8)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 36} = \sqrt{40} = \sqrt{4 \times 10} = 2\sqrt{10}$
  4. $AC = \sqrt{(4-2)^2 + (2-6)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$
  5. Check: $AB^2 + AC^2 = 20 + 20 = 40 = BC^2$, so the triangle is right-angled at $A$ (useful for later parts).
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

(i) $AB = 2\sqrt{5}$ (ii) $BC = 2\sqrt{10}$ (iii) $AC = 2\sqrt{5}$

How the marks are earned

A method mark is earned for one correct use of the distance formula; the remaining marks are accuracy marks for the exact lengths, which should be simplified surds (unsimplified $\sqrt{20}$ may still be accepted as exact, but simplest form is the safe answer).

Calculation check

  • ((6-2) × × 2+(8-6) × × 2) = 20
  • ((4-6) × × 2+(2-8) × × 2) = 40
  • ((4-2) × × 2+(2-6) × × 2) = 20
  • round((2 × 5 × × 0.5) × × 2,6) = 20
  • round((2 × 10 × × 0.5) × × 2,6) = 40
